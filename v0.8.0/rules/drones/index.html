Drones

Running unmanned vehicles from a remote-control deck: jumping into one drone or sitting in the captain’s chair, the network and its subscriber list, the actions a rigger can take, giving a drone orders, and the drones in the core rulebook. What makes a drone (SR3 p.154, p.306)

A drone is any unmanned vehicle that can be run over a rigger network, from a matchbox-sized car to a modified sports car. What sets it apart is its Pilot Rating: its own self-piloting system, which lets it act on its own to a limited degree.

  • A drone must be adapted for rigger control. Drones that can’t carry passengers usually come adapted. A passenger vehicle needs a remote-control interface, 2,500¥ × Body, which gives it a Pilot Rating of 1 and needs Sensor 1 or more (SR3 p.306).
  • It needs a datajack system only if a rigger means to jack into it physically.

Jumped in, or the captain’s chair (SR3 p.154)

Every drone affiliated with the rigger’s deck runs in one of two modes, and the rigger runs the whole network in one of two ways.

  How it works Dice
Primary drone The rigger jumps in: controls it as if jacked directly into it. Only one at a time. The rigger’s skills, plus Combat and Control Pool, and every rigger bonus.
Secondary drones Everything else on the network. The drone’s Pilot Rating in place of skill or Attribute. No pools.
Captain’s chair The rigger jumps into none and supervises them all from the deck’s master control. Every drone is secondary: Pilot Ratings, no pools.
  • While jumped in, the rigger sees through the primary drone. Glancing through a secondary drone’s sensors takes a Complex Action, and orders to secondary drones take +2 on their Comprehension Tests.
  • The captain’s chair is the only way to run drones without a VCR: a character with a datajack and a remote-control deck can do it.

The book’s example (SR3 p.154): Rigger X is in the captain’s chair with three drones when a security transport closes in. He still gets his usual Initiative bonus, but if he orders the drones to attack, none of them gets any of his pool dice, and all roll their Pilot Ratings. He jumps into the roto-drone, the closest: now he can use his Control and Combat Pools for it. Looking through another drone costs a Complex Action, and ordering the Doberman to attack gives it +2 on its Comprehension Test.

The remote-control network (SR3 p.154–156)

A remote-control (RC) deck is the control station, and drones connect to it by radio on three channels: command (movement, actions and information), simsense (sight, sound and simsense) and system (network integrity and drone status).

Subscriber lists (SR3 p.156)

  • The deck only talks to drones on its subscriber list, which keeps intruders out.
  • The list holds twice the deck’s rating in drones; the deck can actively control as many as its rating.
  • A drone under the deck’s control is affiliated; the rigger can see through and command it, and it can talk to the other affiliated drones. A non-affiliated drone acts on its own, cut off from the network.
  • Affiliating or disaffiliating a drone is a Simple Action, so swapping one drone for another takes two Simple Actions or one Complex Action. A drone can’t act in the Combat Turn it’s affiliated.

The book’s example (SR3 p.156): Josie’s Rating 4 deck holds up to 8 drones on its list but controls 4 at once. She has 6 listed and 4 affiliated. To bring in a ground patrol vehicle she spends one Simple Action dropping her surveillance drone and another adding the GPV, which can act from her next Combat Turn.

Range (SR3 p.136–137)

An RC deck has a Flux Rating of 2, a range of 2 km all around it. Situational modifiers change the Flux Rating before the range is read; the modified rating is rounded down to the nearest half.

The book’s example (SR3 p.137–138): Josie’s deck has a signal amplifier, Flux 6. On a garage roof (+4), with encryption (−0.5) and ECCM 3 (−1.5), its effective Flux is 6 + 4 − 0.5 − 1.5 = 8: 20 km.

Jamming, ECM and ECCM against the network are electronic warfare, covered with decking: see Decking.

Giving orders: the Comprehension Test (SR3 p.157)

  • An order is a Complex Action, declared with the rigger’s own actions. One order goes to one drone, or to a group getting the identical order, up to as many as the deck controls.
  • A jumped-in primary drone needs no orders; it’s driven.
  • Orders are one sentence: “circle this area”, “follow that car”. The GM rates the order on the Difficulty Number Table to set the TN, and the rigger can simplify it first.
  • Roll the drone’s Pilot Rating against that TN. One success and it understands and carries the order out; more successes give it more leeway, to the rigger’s benefit.
  • Drones the rigger isn’t driving roll their Pilot Rating for every test, with no dice pools. Every drone acts in the rigger’s Combat Phase, after the rigger.

Sensors (SR3 p.136): a drone making a Passive Sensor Test rolls its Pilot Rating in place of Intelligence; an Active Sensor Test uses its Sensor Rating, as any vehicle does.

Dump shock (SR3 p.156)

Dumped from the network, a rigger is disoriented for ten Combat Turns at +2 to every test, and resists (RC deck rating + 4)S Stun with Willpower. The details are on Rigging.

Doing it in The 2nd Chumming (Foundry)

A drone is a vehicle actor, from the Drones compendiums (Shadowrun 3rd, Rigger 3, State of the Art 2064). Getting it onto a character and its control modes are on Rigging:

  • Auto, a drone’s natural state, rolls its own Pilot Rating.
  • 📡 Drone Comprehension (the drone’s Stats tab, or the Vehicle Tools HUD) rolls the Pilot Rating against the TN the GM sets from the table above, with any electronic-warfare degradation on the command channel added in.
  • VCR is jumped into the primary drone: the rigger rolls their own skills, with Combat and Control Pool. VCR is one vehicle at a time per character, the same as a primary drone.
  • RCD is a secondary drone, or the captain’s chair: the drone’s weapon attacks, Driving Tests and Crash Tests roll its own Pilot Rating, with no pools. A rigger with a VCR keeps their VCR Initiative, as Rigger X does (SR3 p.154); one with only a datajack and a deck rolls ordinary Initiative.

The system doesn’t track subscriber lists or affiliation: the GM keeps count of how many drones the deck is controlling.
