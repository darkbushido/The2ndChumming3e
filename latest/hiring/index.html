Hiring & Service Costs

What the books say about paying shadowrunners, and a house-rule framework for pricing individual specialists. The eight specialist pages below build on this one.

  1. What the books say
    1. Setting the fee (SRComp p.99)
    2. Baseline Shadowrun Payment Table (SRComp p.100)
    3. How Mr. Johnson pays (MJLBB p.20–21)
    4. What living costs (SR3 p.240)
    5. Services with book prices
  2. The house-rule framework

About the original eight hiring gists. They credited their rates to a core-rulebook section called “Spells, Contacts, and Services”. No such section exists, and no SR3 book prices specialists by skill rating. The books price jobs: see the Baseline Payment Table below. The day rates on the specialist pages are kept, marked as house rules, with the example arithmetic corrected. Several of the original examples didn’t add up.

What the books say

Setting the fee (SRComp p.99)

  • Player characters average about one shadowrun a month.
  • A good starting fee for each runner is one month’s living expenses plus the cost of the gear the run needs. If the team’s lifestyles differ, use the average.
  • The GM decides in the end, based on the risk, what the employer can and will pay, and the runners’ reputations.

How to use it (SRComp p.99–100):

  • These are low-end rates for the task alone. More work, more risk, a tougher target or higher-caliber runners should all push them up.
  • Bigger teams can generally expect more. The rates don’t account for team size.
  • The rates don’t include expenses, hazard pay or “death benefits”.
  • In an amoral campaign, where the runners care more about money than doing the right thing, double the base fees and windfalls.
  • A rare windfall should come to roughly six months of the character’s lifestyle.
  • Pay doesn’t have to be cash. Valuable data, equipment, paid expenses, time in a magical research facility, Matrix access and SINs all work.

How Mr. Johnson pays (MJLBB p.20–21)

  • Pay is fluid, and should suit the danger and the style of game. Johnson has a budget he won’t go over, and he lowballs his first offer so there’s room to haggle.
  • The more information and support Johnson provides, the less he’ll pay.
  • Johnson normally covers miscellaneous expenses such as travel and visa fees, if they’re negotiated. Runners who forget to ask may be out of luck.
  • Up-front money: never more than half, and rarely more than a third.
  • Paying in gear: a corporate Johnson can usually afford up to 25% over budget if he pays in equipment. That gear may be tagged, trackable, or a prototype.

The house-rule framework

The specialist pages use this structure. It came from the original gists. The numbers are not from any SR3 book, and are much higher than the Companion’s baselines. For example, a “professional” bodyguard at 1,500¥ a night compares with the book’s 200¥ a day. They fit a high-paying campaign. Scale them to your table.

Fee = base day rate × threat multiplier, plus hazard pay (a percentage of that), plus expenses.

  1. Base day rate is set by the specialist’s tier in their main skill (1–3, 4–5, 6–7, 8+).
  2. Threat multiplier, from about ×1 for routine work to ×3–×6 for heavy opposition.
  3. Hazard pay for being on-site rather than working remotely, usually +50% to +100%.
  4. Expenses and deposits: ammunition, reagents, fake IDs, drone and deck repairs. By the book, Johnson covers expenses only if they’re negotiated (MJLBB p.20–21).

Fixers take a finder’s fee, which the books don’t quantify. The original gists suggested 10–30% depending on how rare the specialist is.

Many top-tier freelancers will ask instead for an equal share of the team’s payout. That fits the Companion’s guidance that the fee is per runner (SRComp p.99).
